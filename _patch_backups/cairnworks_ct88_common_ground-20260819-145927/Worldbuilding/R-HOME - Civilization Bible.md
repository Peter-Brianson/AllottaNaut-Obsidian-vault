# R-HOME

> **Core premise:** Preservation is necessary for their humanity. Expansion is necessary for their survival.

## Canonical Summary

R-HOME is the home reality of [[Elias]] and [[Rowen]], and the name used for the
universe/reality containing their civilization. Their birth world is an
Earth-twin or close Earth-cousin: a living terrestrial planet on which the same
fundamental cores of complex life developed, though not necessarily Earth's
exact species.

Their civilization is considerably older and more technologically advanced than
Baseline humanity, but it is not godlike. Its greatest achievements came from
necessity. The planetary environment that gave birth to the civilization is
slowly failing, particularly through the deterioration of the planet's
protective field. Over immense timescales the world is becoming increasingly
Mars-like.

Their answer was not to surrender the homeworld. It was to expand fast enough
that the species would no longer depend upon a single dying planet.

The result is a mature, multi-layered democratic civilization distributed
through its solar system, regularly extracting material from at least five
planets as well as moons, asteroids, and other dead bodies. Its greatest
undertaking is a Dyson construction network a little over thirty percent
complete. The current structure already produces energy on a scale that makes
Baseline Earth's entire electrical civilization look negligible.

R-HOME is therefore not a utopia and not a fallen civilization. It is a society
that learned, painfully, that survival and preservation are different duties.

---

## The Birth World

The brothers' home planet should feel immediately recognizable as a cousin of
Earth:

- oceans, continents, weather, forests, microbial ecologies, and complex animal life;
- a long natural and civilizational history;
- familiar physical vulnerability despite extraordinary technology;
- regions of astonishing restoration beside scars from earlier industrial eras.

The world is farther along in **erosion, complexity, and age** than Baseline
Earth. Its protective planetary field is deteriorating. Atmospheric loss,
radiation exposure, environmental instability, and other long-duration effects
are slowly making continued planetary dependence untenable.

This is not an apocalypse that arrives on one terrible afternoon.

**Their apocalypse is a measurement.**

Each generation can observe that the planet is a little less capable of
protecting the generations that will follow.

The species may survive the loss of its birthplace. Its history cannot simply be
replaced.

### Cultural response

R-HOME did not conclude that a dying planet was disposable. It concluded almost
the opposite:

**"Because it is still here."**

A forest that may someday disappear is still restored.
A damaged coastline is still cleaned.
An abandoned industrial basin is still reclaimed.
A habitat expected to survive only another thousand years is still worth
protecting.

Impermanence does not make something worthless.

## Expansion as Species Survival

The civilization's rapid solar expansion was brute-forced by necessity.

Its technological development is consequently uneven. Technologies directly
connected to survival, infrastructure, energy, transport, materials, and
habitation received extraordinary attention. Technologies that were merely
interesting did not necessarily progress at the same rate.

A useful shorthand for the civilization's developmental path is:

**energy -> extraction -> propulsion -> orbital construction -> closed habitats -> stellar infrastructure**

Near-light-speed travel became essential not merely for exploration but for
ecology and industry. Once the civilization could economically reach distant
dead worlds, it could stop stripping so much material from the living one.

The breakthrough was effectively:

**Stop digging here. Go somewhere else.**

Expansion and preservation therefore became complementary rather than opposing
ideologies.

## Extraction Network

R-HOME regularly uses at least **five planets** for material extraction, with
additional mining throughout moons, asteroids, dwarf bodies, and other suitable
objects.

Preferred industrial targets are worlds that are already dead or nearly dead:

- Mars-like terrestrial planets;
- airless or sparsely atmospheric moons;
- metal-rich asteroids and minor bodies;
- icy outer-system bodies for water and industrial feedstocks;
- other geologically useful worlds where large-scale extraction causes little
  or no biological loss.

### Gas giants remain hostile

Their technology does not make nature irrelevant.

Jupiter- and Saturn-like worlds are not treated as convenient mines. Extreme
gravity, crushing pressure, violent atmospheric systems, corrosive chemistry,
and immense storms remain dangerous. Acid is still acid. Hurricanes can still
shred equipment.

Specialized atmospheric harvesting may exist where worthwhile, but "technically
possible" and "economically sensible" remain different things.

This is an important technological rule for R-HOME:

**They are not masters of nature. They have become very good at choosing where
to push against it.**

## The Old Extraction Scar

Before off-world industry became practical, the civilization mined its
birthplace brutally to obtain the material necessary for survival and expansion.

Mountains, crust, ocean floors, and enormous industrial zones were exploited to
build the first generations of orbital infrastructure.

This creates one of R-HOME's central historical ironies:

**They damaged their dying world while building the infrastructure that would
allow them to stop damaging it.**

Modern generations live with the evidence of that era. Some landscapes remain
permanently altered. Others are being restored. Old industrial scars are not
necessarily hidden; some may be preserved as reminders of what desperation
cost.

## Preservation and Reclamation

Preservation is not a fashionable political position in modern R-HOME. It is a
fundamental component of civic humanity.

Large organizations are expected to remove industrial material and reclaim
zones they no longer use.

Their basic construction philosophy is:

**Build -> Use -> Recover**

A project is not considered finished merely because it has stopped producing.
Its responsibility ends when the site has been returned to an agreed condition.

Recovery may include:

- removal of machinery and hazardous material;
- reclamation of reusable metals and structural components;
- dismantling unwanted foundations;
- stabilization of damaged terrain;
- restoration of water movement;
- replacement or reconstruction of soils where practical;
- reintroduction of vegetation and habitat;
- retention of infrastructure the local population actually wants.

Perfect ecological restoration is not always possible. Responsibility still is.

### Abandoned industry as waste

To a citizen of R-HOME, an abandoned factory is not only ugly. It is an
unrecovered stockpile.

**"Why would you leave all that metal there? We need metal."**

The enormous stellar construction economy makes reclamation materially useful.
Old buildings, rails, industrial platforms, ships, processing machinery, and
power systems can become feedstock for newer construction.

Old civilization can physically become new civilization.

A beam once holding up a planetary refinery might generations later become part
of a solar collector.

### Civic dignity

The society is particularly hostile to the normalization of abandoned
industrial districts around poorer populations.

A common moral argument is:

**The condition in which society leaves a place demonstrates what society thinks
of the people who remain there.**

R-HOME has not necessarily eliminated poverty, corruption, negligence, or
inequality. Companies can still attempt to evade cleanup costs and governments
can still fail.

The civilization became responsible not because everyone became good, but
because irresponsible people repeatedly taught society which laws it needed.

## Government: The Council System

R-HOME is inherently democratic, in part because its population and physical
scale make simple centralized governance impractical.

Government is divided into many necessary sectors and layers, conceptually
descended from the same pressures that cause modern governments to divide
responsibilities among departments, agencies, regional governments, local
governments, regulatory bodies, and representative institutions.

At R-HOME's scale, this has developed into a broad **Council system**.

Possible domains include:

- stellar infrastructure;
- planetary and orbital infrastructure;
- energy generation and distribution;
- transport and navigation;
- extraction and resource stewardship;
- ecological preservation and reclamation;
- habitation and migration;
- scientific research;
- biological and medical continuity;
- ethics;
- cultural memory;
- exploration;
- public safety and security;
- local and regional governance.

These are not necessarily the final canonical names.

### Overlapping authority

No single Council needs to "own" the Dyson project.

A stellar collector may simultaneously involve infrastructure, energy,
extraction, transportation, ecology, labor, finance, research, and local
authorities.

Councils negotiate.

They disagree.

They overlap.

They can be slow.

Their democracy should feel like a civilization-scale network of agreements,
not a perfect machine and not a single parliament that somehow votes on every
problem in the solar system.

### Boards of advisors

Specialized boards of advisors can exist beneath or alongside Councils,
especially where technical, scientific, commercial, and civic interests overlap.

Elias and Rowen occasionally volunteer for expeditions associated with one such
board. They are not rulers, elite government agents, or chosen heroes. They are
contracted working people whose interests and qualifications occasionally make
them useful participants.

## The Dyson Construction

R-HOME's most advanced technology is its Dyson project.

It is best understood as an enormous evolving **Dyson swarm / distributed solar
collection architecture**, with a distant ambition of becoming something far
more complete and structurally integrated.

At the brothers' era it is a little over **30% complete relative to the
civilization's intended mature architecture**.

It is already enormous.

The project may still be centuries or a millennium or more from anything
resembling its final form.

### What "30%" means

Thirty percent should not require a literal solid shell covering thirty percent
of the stellar surface.

It represents progress toward the civilization's intended total stellar
infrastructure: collectors, relays, habitats, construction systems, industrial
platforms, orbital management systems, and eventually more contiguous
structures.

The exact engineering can remain deliberately obscure.

The audience needs proof of capability, not a textbook.

### Existing output

Even incomplete, the network produces **billions of times more usable electrical
energy than Baseline Earth civilization**.

The number itself should rarely matter in dialogue.

The consequences matter:

- enormous orbital industries can function continuously;
- planetary cities can receive stellar-derived power;
- energy-intensive refining can be moved away from the homeworld;
- giant research systems and particle accelerators are practical;
- more collectors can be built faster.

### The self-accelerating loop

The Dyson project helps build the Dyson project:

**collectors -> energy -> extraction -> refining -> manufacturing -> transport
-> orbital construction -> more collectors**

At some point this feedback loop became one of the civilization's most important
historical thresholds.

Early R-HOME consumed its homeworld to reach space.

Space eventually supplied enough resources to stop consuming home.

The star then began supplying enough energy to accelerate the process.

## Wireless Stellar Power

R-HOME has developed imperfect but practical methods of transmitting large
amounts of collected energy without continuous physical wiring.

The exact mechanism does not need dense scientific jargon.

Conceptually:

1. A collector captures stellar energy.
2. A transmitter converts and directs it.
3. A receiver intercepts the transmission.
4. The receiving infrastructure converts it into useful local power.

Large transmission relays therefore form part of the solar infrastructure.

The technology has limitations:

- energy is lost;
- alignment matters;
- vacuum transmission is easier than transmission through atmosphere;
- receivers for major loads can be enormous;
- obstruction or equipment failure can interrupt delivery;
- high-output transmission systems are inherently dangerous.

A system capable of powering a city could also cause catastrophic damage if
deliberately or accidentally misdirected. Large-scale redirection therefore
requires strong regulation, redundant safeguards, and distributed authorization.

A simple line can communicate more than jargon:

**"I can't redirect that array by myself."**

## Technology Level and Aesthetic

The intended feel is approximately **Star Trek capability with a Star Wars
texture**, without copying either setting.

Technology is extremely advanced but lived-in.

Ships can show wear.
Tools remain tools.
Interfaces can be elegant without making physical controls obsolete.
Infrastructure can be repaired rather than magically replaced.
Technology belongs to ordinary workers as much as scientists.

The series should generally favor **proof of concept over science jargon**.

If the audience sees Rowen use near-light travel as part of an ordinary
transport contract, they understand the civilization without hearing a lecture
about the drive.

### Major technological strengths

R-HOME is particularly advanced in:

- Dyson-scale stellar construction;
- near-light-speed travel;
- orbital and interplanetary infrastructure;
- particle acceleration and high-energy physics;
- nuclear science;
- advanced power transmission;
- material extraction and processing;
- closed or semi-closed habitats;
- medicine and healthy-life extension.

Nuclear technology is mature but is gradually losing its role as a dominant
energy source as stellar energy becomes increasingly available.

### Important limitations

R-HOME does **not** possess:

- practical time travel;
- casual resurrection;
- immortality;
- unlimited matter creation;
- effortless teleportation across arbitrary distances;
- immunity to radiation, pressure, gravity, corrosive chemistry, starvation,
  isolation, or industrial accidents;
- scientific understanding equivalent to AllottaNaut's internal authority.

Distance, matter, energy, environment, maintenance, and death still matter.

## Teleportation

Teleportation exists as a scientific and ethical frontier rather than a solved
convenience.

Its limitations and ethics are persistently debated.

The central philosophical problem is continuity:

If a person is disassembled and reconstructed elsewhere, did that person travel,
or did one person die while an exact successor appeared?

Cargo applications and narrowly defined emergency uses may be more accepted than
routine human transportation.

The technology is intentionally less mature and culturally less comfortable
than ordinary space travel.

R-HOME's scientists are advanced enough to make teleportation plausible and
still human enough to ask whether they should use it.

## Longevity

R-HOME has significantly extended human longevity without defeating aging.

A useful current scale:

- Baseline Earth comparison: life expectancy roughly 75 years, exceptional
  maximum roughly 120-130.
- R-HOME life expectancy: roughly **105-115 years**.
- Exceptional R-HOME upper limit: potentially **200-250 years** under unusually
  favorable biology, treatment, environment, and luck.

The maximum is not the average.

A 250-year potential lifespan does not prevent someone from dying at 36.

### Why longevity became necessary

R-HOME's infrastructure projects outgrew the natural continuity of human
expertise.

It can take ten, twenty, or thirty years to produce an expert capable of safely
maintaining some of civilization's most complicated systems. Losing that person
after only a few productive decades creates enormous institutional and
technical costs.

Longevity research therefore became partially an infrastructure necessity:

**It takes decades to teach someone how to keep this running. We cannot keep
starting over.**

The civilization studied long-lived organisms - including analogues of
tortoises, sea turtles, whales, and other exceptionally long-lived creatures -
for biological clues about cellular maintenance, cancer resistance, DNA repair,
inflammation, tissue regeneration, and slow accumulation of age-related damage.

No single "immortality gene" is required. Medicine simply became progressively
better at maintaining a human body.

### Longer life is not invulnerability

People still die from:

- radiation;
- explosions;
- decompression and industrial accidents;
- severe gravity-related illness or injury;
- starvation when remote expansion infrastructure fails;
- cancer and other diseases medicine cannot always stop;
- loneliness;
- depression;
- psychological collapse during prolonged isolation;
- ordinary accidents;
- eventual aging.

Longer life amplifies some emotional problems rather than solving them.

A person may outlive a spouse by a century.
An explorer can be physically healthy and profoundly isolated.
A worker can spend eighty years contributing to a structure they will never see
completed.

### Labor ethics

Early longevity programs may have carried an ugly utilitarian argument: trained
specialists were expensive to replace, so extending their productive years was
valuable.

Modern R-HOME has had time to wrestle with the ethical consequence.

**Longer life means longer personhood, not longer servitude.**

Long careers therefore need not mean doing one job for 150 uninterrupted years.
People may work for decades, leave a field, teach, rest, retrain, raise families,
volunteer, or begin entirely different careers.

A forty-year career can be one chapter of a life.

## Elias

Elias is fundamentally a working engineer, not R-HOME's greatest scientist.

His contracted work overlaps with the civilization's enormous infrastructure
network. His exact specialization can remain flexible enough for story needs,
but his experience plausibly touches orbital systems, construction,
maintenance, power infrastructure, remote installations, and the practical
interfaces between complicated systems.

His strength is not knowing everything.

It is knowing how to approach something that is not behaving correctly.

His professional instinct is:

**Observe -> isolate -> test -> understand -> stabilize**

This naturally foreshadows his role in teaching the infant AllottaNaut
structure, physics, causality, history, boundaries, and stabilization.

Elias can understand the systems he works on without knowing how every reactor,
drive, particle system, or medical technology in his civilization works.

He is an engineer, not an encyclopedia.

---

## Rowen

Rowen is a **commercial transporter**.

He is part of the circulatory system of R-HOME.

His work can involve moving:

- raw materials;
- construction components;
- scientific equipment;
- personnel;
- supplies;
- specialist cargo;
- materials between extraction worlds, moons, orbital yards, habitats, and
  construction networks.

Near-light travel is therefore not mystical to Rowen.

It is part of his job.

He may treat a machine astonishing to Baseline humanity with the familiarity of
a professional driver checking a work vehicle before a route.

His profession also exposes him to people throughout the civilization: dock
workers, miners, engineers, scientists, colonists, contractors, families,
station residents, officials, and other transport crews.

This supports the relational instincts he later brings to AllottaNaut.

A useful contrast:

**Elias asks: "How does this work?"**

**Rowen asks: "Where does this need to go, and who is waiting for it?"**

## The Brothers as Ordinary People

Elias and Rowen need jobs.

That is important.

They are not born as legendary explorers, generals, prophets, or the two most
important scientists in their reality.

They are ordinary members of an extraordinary civilization.

Their jobs overlap naturally:

**Elias helps build and maintain the infrastructure.  
Rowen helps make the infrastructure useful.**

Elias might commission a remote extraction system.
Rowen might bring its machinery in and carry its first processed material out.

Both can be contractors working within the enormous public/private/cooperative
ecosystem surrounding the Councils rather than permanent government officials.

They still have schedules, certifications, supervisors, inspections, contracts,
maintenance, budgets, and paperwork.

The scale has changed.

Human work has not disappeared.

## Curiosity and Volunteer Expeditions

Both brothers are fascinated by what may exist beyond what R-HOME already
understands.

They also deeply love home.

Exploration is not rejection of home.

Rowen can love leaving partly because he loves returning.
Elias can want to understand the universe partly because knowledge might help
preserve what his civilization still has.

In their spare time they study sciences out of genuine interest.

Because R-HOME humans can live longer, broad amateur expertise is plausible.
Someone who spends thirty years casually studying astronomy can know a great
deal of astronomy without being an astronomer.

The brothers occasionally volunteer for expeditions associated with their board
of advisors.

Possible assignments include:

- survey missions;
- unusual astronomical observations;
- testing experimental equipment;
- investigating remote phenomena;
- visiting newly accessible bodies;
- scientific support work beyond routine commercial routes.

Their eventual encounter with the anomaly should not require them to begin as
chosen heroes.

A board can simply need a capable engineer, an experienced transporter, suitable
equipment, and curious volunteers.

They say yes.

Then they find something their civilization has no category for.

## Cultural Tone

R-HOME should be advanced in an **obscure** way.

The audience should not constantly be told how advanced it is. They should see
advanced things treated as ordinary.

The setting should retain the core tonal priorities of AllottaNaut:

- philosophy;
- emotion;
- whimsy;
- reflection;
- curiosity;
- human-scale relationships inside enormous concepts.

The civilization can be technically astonishing without becoming sterile.

Its citizens still joke.
They still get tired.
They still love home.
They still argue with bureaucracy.
They still worry about family.
They still take pride in work.
They still make bad decisions.
They still grieve.

Advancement has not eliminated humanity.

## Preservation and Expansion: The Central Duality

R-HOME's civilization is built around two truths:

### Preservation is necessary for their humanity.

Without preservation, survival becomes mere continuation.

They preserve ecosystems, landscapes, cities, history, knowledge, relationships,
and the dignity of inhabited places because these things answer the question of
*what* is surviving.

### Expansion is necessary for their survival.

Without expansion, the deterioration of their birth world eventually wins.

They therefore mine dead worlds, build habitats, cross astronomical distances,
construct around their star, and distribute civilization beyond a single
planet.

These are not rival philosophies.

**Preservation tells them what not to lose.  
Expansion gives them somewhere to carry it.**

## Encountering AllottaNaut

The eventual return of Elias and Rowen creates enormous friction because
AllottaNaut does not merely appear to possess more advanced technology.

It appears to violate R-HOME's concept of what technology *is*.

R-HOME's greatest accomplishments all came from respecting physical
constraints.

They learned to work with matter.
They learned to harvest energy.
They learned to cross distance.
They learned to slow biological deterioration.
They learned to reclaim what they damaged.

AllottaNaut confronts them with something closer to **authority over the
conditions themselves**.

Questions from R-HOME scientists quickly become uncomfortable:

"What powers it?"

"Where is its mass?"

"How large is it?"

"How does it prevent entropy?"

"Where does the air come from?"

"What mechanism maintains the stasis?"

And eventually Elias or Rowen must give an answer that sounds intolerable to a
scientist:

**"AllottaNaut does."**

The conversation begins as engineering and starts sounding like theology.

---

## Why the Deity Comparison Becomes Inevitable

R-HOME can imagine technology beyond its own.

An advanced alien machine might be incomprehensible today yet theoretically
understandable someday.

AllottaNaut is different if the internal state of its reality derives from its
own authority.

R-HOME's philosophers and theologians may recognize the problem before its
physicists:

**"You are describing properties our traditions historically reserved for
gods."**

That does not require AllottaNaut to claim divinity.

AllottaNaut may simply answer:

**"I am AllottaNaut."**

Everyone else has the existential crisis.

---

## Why R-HOME Is Especially Vulnerable to the Discovery

R-HOME's civilization was shaped by erosion.

Its planet is dying.
Its people age.
Its experts die.
Its infrastructure requires maintenance.
Its greatest construction may take a millennium.
Its expansion workers still die from radiation, explosions, gravity, starvation,
and isolation.
Its preservation culture exists because damage has consequences.

One of the civilization's deepest learned assumptions is:

**Everything has a cost. Everything deteriorates. Preserve what you can.**

Then Elias and Rowen return with evidence of a place where **erosion can be
optional**.

For R-HOME, that is not merely scientific.

It is existential.

A grieving parent may ask:
**"Could it have saved my child?"**

A widow:
**"Could it have preserved my spouse?"**

An engineer:
**"Could we put the planet inside?"**

A preservationist:
**"Could it preserve the biosphere?"**

A medical researcher:
**"Can its stasis be reproduced?"**

A security council:
**"Can anything destroy it?"**

A physicist:
**"Can we build one?"**

A religious thinker:
**"Did we discover it, or did it choose us?"**

And inevitably:

**"Why do Elias and Rowen get to decide who speaks to it?"**

## The Political Problem After R-HOME FOUND

The Councils do not need to become a stereotypical evil government.

The more believable conflict is:

**Everybody has legitimate reasons to want jurisdiction.**

Science wants access.
Infrastructure wants cooperation.
Preservationists want help for the homeworld.
Medicine wants to understand stasis.
Exploration wants to understand its reach.
Ethics wants limits.
Security wants contingency plans.
Religious communities want dialogue.
The public wants answers.

Elias and Rowen suddenly become enormously important despite never seeking that
position.

They are an engineer and a transporter who happen to possess the longest human
relationship with something their civilization cannot classify.

Their most important defense of AllottaNaut may be extremely simple:

**"You can ask."**

Not:
"You cannot study it."

Not:
"You cannot work with it."

But:
**"It is a person. Ask."**

R-HOME is accustomed to studying phenomena.

AllottaNaut can refuse.

That distinction can become one of the central political and philosophical
conflicts of the post-return story.

## The Brothers' Perspective

To R-HOME, AllottaNaut may be:

- the greatest scientific discovery in history;
- a possible solution to planetary decline;
- an unprecedented security concern;
- a new category of life;
- evidence with religious implications;
- a possible route around erosion itself.

To Elias and Rowen:

**AllottaNaut is someone they know.**

That difference is the heart of the conflict.

## Relationship to AllottaNaut's Development

R-HOME provides an important cultural inheritance for the infant AllottaNaut.

Elias teaches concepts associated with:

- physics;
- cause and effect;
- history;
- structure;
- boundaries;
- stabilization;
- mapping;
- understanding.

Rowen teaches concepts associated with:

- emotion;
- morality;
- fun;
- attachment;
- listening;
- resonance;
- relationship;
- willingness.

Their civilization contributes another principle through both brothers:

**If you change a place, you become responsible for what your change leaves
behind.**

At its simplest:

**We clean up after ourselves.**

For R-HOME this began as environmental and civic responsibility.

For AllottaNaut, which may eventually enter realities, alter environments,
preserve civilizations, contain dangerous beings, reconstruct patterns, and
exercise authority over erosion, that childhood lesson may someday become a
cosmic principle of stewardship.

## Visual and Experiential Notes

R-HOME should not look like a uniformly polished science-fiction utopia.

The birth world can contain:

- carefully restored forests growing over former industrial zones;
- old extraction scars too large to erase;
- reclaimed cities;
- preserved historical machinery;
- orbital structures visible in the sky;
- stellar collectors appearing like artificial constellations;
- power infrastructure feeding cities from off-world generation;
- launch and transport systems integrated into ordinary life;
- beautiful districts alongside places still recovering from old mistakes.

Advanced technology should feel mundane to the people using it.

A worker complaining about a maintenance delay can communicate more than a page
of exposition.

A child growing up believing abandoned factories are bizarre can show how far
the culture has moved.

An elder remembering when the homeworld was still routinely mined can show how
recent that moral transformation actually is.

## Design Rules

When writing R-HOME:

1. **Show capability through ordinary use.** Avoid unnecessary science jargon.
2. **Keep physics consequential.** Advanced technology does not erase danger.
3. **Do not make R-HOME a utopia.** Its ethics were learned through mistakes.
4. **Do not make the Councils automatically sinister.** Institutional conflict
   can arise from competing legitimate responsibilities.
5. **Keep Elias and Rowen ordinary at the beginning.** Their significance comes
   from the encounter, not predestination.
6. **Preserve the preservation/expansion duality.** Neither works without the
   other.
7. **Treat longevity as maintenance, not immortality.**
8. **Let technology remain tactile, used, repaired, regulated, and lived-in.**
9. **AllottaNaut should feel categorically strange even to R-HOME.**
10. **The civilization's greatest gift to AllottaNaut may be stewardship, not
    science.**

## One-Sentence Identity

**R-HOME is a democratic, system-spanning human civilization that scarred its
dying Earth-like birthplace to escape it, then used the stars to preserve what
remained - only for two ordinary brothers to encounter a being for whom erosion
itself may be a choice.**
